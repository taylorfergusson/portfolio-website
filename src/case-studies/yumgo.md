## Overview

<p class="my-part">Led a team of 4, from research to the tested prototype</p>

### Providing comfort in unfamiliar places

YumGo is a food discovery app for tourists. When you're somewhere new, with dietary needs or a certain vibe in mind, it helps you find a place that feels right before you walk in.

<div class="summary">

- **!The problem** Finding restaurant details often means bouncing between apps, with missing menus and info you can't trust.
- **Why it matters** Decision-making quickly becomes stressful on top of other travel pressures.
- **+The solution** One app for the whole journey, from discovering to dining, so you know what to expect before you sit down.

</div>

## Problem

### Eating while travelling should be good and easy. No ifs, ands, or buts.

Unfamiliarity is often what creates such novel and exciting travel experiences. It's all fun and games, until you're tired, short on time, juggling a group… and then hunger hits.

Now you're bouncing between apps to find the info you actually care about. Where do you start if you can't even think straight?

> **Problem Statement**
> Tourists need a personalized and centralized way to discover and book restaurants while abroad, so that their travel experiences can be memorable, culturally-immersive, high-value, and easy.

## Secondary research

<p class="my-part">Convinced the team to pivot from speed to authenticity</p>

### We (wrongfully) assumed it was all about time

We started out with the assumption that tourists wanted food ASAP so they could get back to sightseeing. Early research suggested otherwise, since wait times barely came up.

- ![](/images/yumgo/icon-missing-info.png) **!Missing information** Incomplete business and menu info
- ![](/images/yumgo/icon-weak-discovery.png) **!Weak discovery & booking** Apps didn't support the full journey
- ![](/images/yumgo/icon-irrelevant.png) **!Irrelevant content** Full of noise and unwanted results
- ![](/images/yumgo/icon-authenticity.png) **+Desire for authenticity!** Tourists wanted local, quality meals

Not everyone was ready to let go of the original idea, but I emphasized how it's important to move in the direction that the research is pushing us.

> **The takeaway**
> We shifted gears away from wait times and toward the authentic experiences that weren't showcased in current apps.

## Interviews

<p class="my-part">Co-wrote the guides, moderated 2 of 8 interviews, and ran our affinity mapping</p>

### Travellers wanted to dine with certainty

We ran eight interviews with recent tourists about travel, food, and the apps they use for both travel and food. I co-wrote the screener and interview guide, moderated two interviews, and took notes on two more. Then I ran our affinity mapping session to pull everything together.

![Affinity map from our interviews](/images/yumgo/research-1.jpg)

On the surface, people talked about menus, reviews, and photos, but thematic analysis revealed that they all wanted a sense of familiarity in an unfamiliar place.

> **The takeaway**
> People wanted to feel like they'd been somewhere before they walked in the door, so we focused on cultural connection and seeing the menu ahead of time.

## Testing

<p class="my-part">Rebuilt the broken filters between tests</p>

### Clarity had to be improved… a lot

I co-wrote our usability test guide, and eight participants tried two tasks in our Figma prototype:

1. Book a table somewhere dog-friendly with vegan and gluten-free options
2. Write a review afterwards

<div class="callout-box">

**MAYDAY**

Task 1 sent people straight to the search filters, which was full of broken toggles, and both of our first two participants got stuck here. OH NO!

I knew that if we left it, a big portion of testing time would be wasted revealing something we already knew. Since I knew Figma's variables and components best out of the team, I tackled the problem in the couple of hours before the next session. I successfully rebuilt the filters' variables, components, and interactions so that every toggle responded as expected.

#### What we discovered

- ![](/images/yumgo/icon-filters.png) **!Filters were unclear** People still didn't know what the icons meant
- ![](/images/yumgo/icon-key-info.png) **!Key info was easy to miss** Too much text buried what mattered

<p class="kicker">The fix · Labels for every icon, and less text</p>

## Design system

<p class="my-part">Set up the tokens, components and documentation</p>

### Design system

When I polished YumGo for my portfolio, I rebuilt the components and styles on a full design system.

<div class="numbered">

1. **Reusable restaurant page for all restaurants** I used variable modes to swap the content on the restaurant page, keeping consistency between 5 restaurants.
2. **31 component sets** Tab bars, headers, footers, filter chips, and more, each with descriptive variant states.
3. **AA contrast throughout** Every text pairing is at least 4.5:1, and every icon has a text label.

## Reflection

### What I learned

<div class="numbered">

1. **Taking on too much work isn't leading** Halfway through, I caught myself rewriting teammates' sections as we pulled things together, which made me the bottleneck. So I moved the work earlier: we agreed on direction, scope, and reasons before anyone started drafting, and I kept it all in one shared brief (our findings, what we were still working on, and what we'd dropped). Whenever the work started drifting off-scope, the brief brought us back in.
2. **Leadership is hard to judge from the inside** While I tried my best to be a "good leader", there were times I felt like I wasn't leading the team well, like when we felt confused or took the project in different directions. At the end, one teammate told me unprompted that I was a good leader, which meant a lot. This taught me to check in more with my peers, not for reassurance, but to make sure they are getting what they need.
3. **Moving in reverse is still moving** It was hard to backpedal on wait times, features, and stylistic directions. However, I learned to hold ideas loosely and see pivots for what they really are: improvements!

</div>
