## Overview

<p class="my-part">Led a team of 4, from research to tested prototype</p>

### Comfort in the unfamiliar

YumGo is a food discovery app for tourists. When you're somewhere new, with dietary needs or a certain vibe in mind, it helps you find a place that feels right before you walk in.

<div class="summary">

- **!The problem** Finding somewhere to eat on a trip means bouncing between apps, with missing menus and info you can't trust.
- **Why it matters** Being somewhere new is half the fun of travelling, until you're tired, juggling a group, and hunger hits.
- **+The solution** One app for the whole journey, from discovering to dining, so you know what to expect before you sit down.

</div>

## Problem

### Eating while travelling should be good and easy. No ifs, ands, or buts.

Being somewhere unfamiliar is half the fun of travelling. It's all fun and games, until you're tired, short on time, juggling a group… and then hunger hits.

Now you're bouncing between apps to find the info you actually care about. Where do you even start?

> **Problem Statement**
> Tourists need one personalized place to discover and book restaurants, so that eating somewhere new feels easy and memorable.

## Secondary research

<p class="my-part">Convinced the team to pivot from speed to authenticity</p>

### We (wrongfully) assumed it was all about time

We started out sure that tourists wanted food ASAP so they could get back to sightseeing. Early research said otherwise: wait times barely came up.

- ![](/images/yumgo/icon-missing-info.png) **!Missing information** Incomplete business and menu info
- ![](/images/yumgo/icon-weak-discovery.png) **!Weak discovery & booking** Apps didn't support the full journey
- ![](/images/yumgo/icon-irrelevant.png) **!Irrelevant content** Full of noise and unwanted results
- ![](/images/yumgo/icon-authenticity.png) **+Desire for authenticity!** Tourists wanted local, quality meals

Not everyone was ready to let go of the original idea. When a pitch doesn't land, I assume I didn't explain it well enough yet, so I walked the team back through what the research actually said instead of arguing my opinion.

> **The takeaway**
> So we pivoted: less about speed, more about the authentic experiences current apps weren't supporting.

## Interviews

<p class="my-part">Co-wrote the guides, moderated 2 of 8 interviews, and ran our affinity mapping</p>

### Travellers wanted to dine with certainty

We ran eight interviews with recent tourists about travel, food, and the apps they use for it. I co-wrote the screener and interview guide, moderated two interviews, and took notes on two more. Then I ran our affinity mapping session to pull everything together.

On the surface, people talked about menus, reviews, and photos. Grouped into higher-level themes, they were all saying the same thing: they wanted a sense of familiarity in an unfamiliar place.

> **The takeaway**
> People wanted to feel like they'd been somewhere before they walked in the door. So we focused on cultural connection and seeing the menu ahead of time.

## Testing

<p class="my-part">Rebuilt the broken filters between tests</p>

### Clarity had to be improved… a lot

I co-wrote our test guide, and eight participants tried two tasks in our Figma prototype, on their phones:

1. Book a table somewhere dog-friendly with vegan and gluten-free options
2. Write a review afterwards

<div class="callout-box">

**MAYDAY**

Task 1 sent people straight to the search filters, and the toggles were broken. Both of our first two participants got stuck, and we had to step in and guide them through. OH NO!

If we left it, every test after that would just tell us the same thing we already knew. I was the one on the team who knew Figma's variables and components best, so in the couple of hours before the next session, I rebuilt the filters' variables, components, and interactions so every toggle actually responded.

## Reflection

### What I learned

<div class="numbered">

1. **Fixing everyone's work isn't leading** Halfway through, I caught myself rewriting teammates' sections as we pulled things together, which made me the bottleneck. So I moved the work earlier: we agreed on direction, scope, and reasons before anyone started drafting, and I kept it all in one shared brief (our findings, what we were still working on, and what we'd dropped). When someone's AI-assisted draft wandered off-scope, the brief brought it back.
2. **Disagreements need evidence** We talked every disagreement through, with one rule: a new direction had to point back to something we'd found in research. That kept the arguing about the work, not about who was right.
3. **Moving in reverse is still moving** Backpedalling on wait times was hard, but I learned to hold ideas loosely and see pivots for what they REALLY are: improvements!

</div>
